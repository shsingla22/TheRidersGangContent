The Polo Shirt: From Horseback to High Street

By The Rider's Gang | March 5, 2026 | 5 min read

Somewhere in the world, right now, someone is putting on a polo shirt. It might be a hedge fund manager in Manhattan, a student in Lagos, a retiree in Osaka, or a factory worker in Dhaka. The polo shirt is worn by more people, in more countries, in more contexts than almost any other garment on earth. An estimated two billion are sold every year. It is the great democratic shirt — classless, ageless, borderless.

And it was born on horseback. The polo shirt owes its name, its shape, and its cultural meaning to a sport played by riders on galloping horses. This is the story of how a game from the high plateaus of Central Asia gave the world its most versatile piece of clothing.

The Fields of India: Where It All Started

Polo is one of the oldest team sports in the world. It originated in Persia — modern-day Iran — more than two thousand years ago, where it was played by nomadic warriors as a form of cavalry training. The game spread east along the Silk Road to China, Japan, and the Indian subcontinent. In the mountainous regions of Manipur and Gilgit, in what is now north-east India and northern Pakistan, local horsemen played a version of the game they called "pulu" — the Balti word for the willow root from which the ball was carved.

In the 1850s, British officers stationed in India discovered the game and fell in love with it. They organised the first formal polo clubs, wrote rules, and began shipping the sport back to England. By the 1870s, polo was being played at Hurlingham in London, and it quickly became the sport of the aristocracy — fast, dangerous, and expensive. You needed at least four horses to play a single match. Only the wealthy could afford it.

But there was a practical problem. British officers and gentlemen of the Victorian era dressed formally — even for sport. Early polo players wore long-sleeved shirts with stiff, starched collars. On horseback, at full gallop, swinging a mallet, this was miserable. The collars flapped in the wind, caught on things, and chafed the neck. The long sleeves were hot and restrictive. Something had to change.

"The polo shirt exists because Victorian gentlemen insisted on playing a violent sport on horseback while dressed as if they were going to a dinner party. Practicality won in the end — it always does."

Lacoste, Brooks Brothers, and the Birth of the Modern Shirt

The polo shirt as we know it today was not actually invented by a polo player. It was invented by a tennis player — but polo gave it the name that stuck.

In the 1920s, the French tennis champion René Lacoste was frustrated with the standard tennis uniform: a long-sleeved, button-up dress shirt with rolled sleeves. It was too hot, too stiff, and it restricted his movement on the court. So Lacoste designed his own shirt. It had short sleeves, a soft ribbed collar, a longer back hem (to keep it tucked in during play), and was made from a breathable cotton mesh fabric called piqué. He wore it at the 1926 US Open and caused a sensation.

In 1933, Lacoste and a business partner, André Gillier, began mass-producing the shirt. It featured a small embroidered crocodile on the chest — Lacoste's nickname was "the Crocodile" because of a bet involving an alligator-skin suitcase. It was the first piece of clothing to carry a visible brand logo on the outside. Every branded polo shirt since — every Ralph Lauren pony, every Fred Perry laurel wreath, every Nike swoosh — descends from that tiny crocodile.

Meanwhile, in America, Brooks Brothers was approaching the same problem from a different direction. Their designers had noticed that polo players in England buttoned their shirt collars down to stop them flapping during matches. In the 1930s, Brooks Brothers created a casual shirt with a button-down collar inspired directly by polo players. They called it the "polo shirt." The name stuck — not to their button-down, but to the soft-collared, short-sleeved design that Lacoste had pioneered. The two ideas merged in the public imagination, and the polo shirt was born.

"René Lacoste made the shirt. Polo players gave it the name. Between them, they created the most worn garment of the twentieth century."

Ralph Lauren and the Polo Empire

If Lacoste invented the polo shirt, Ralph Lauren turned it into an empire. In 1967, a young designer from the Bronx named Ralph Lifshitz — he would later change his surname to Lauren — started a necktie business. He had a vision: to sell not just clothing, but a lifestyle. And the lifestyle he chose was the world of the horse.

In 1972, Lauren introduced the polo shirt into his collection. It came in twenty-four colours, it had a soft mesh fabric, and it carried a small embroidered logo on the left chest: a polo player on horseback, mallet raised. The brand was called Polo Ralph Lauren. Everything about it — the name, the logo, the advertising — evoked the world of polo: wealth, horses, open fields, old money, and effortless elegance.

Lauren had never played polo in his life. But he understood something profound: the sport of polo carried a set of associations — privilege, tradition, athleticism, and the outdoors — that people wanted to wear on their bodies. The polo shirt became the vehicle for that aspiration. When you wore a Ralph Lauren polo, you weren't just wearing a shirt. You were wearing the idea of a life lived on horseback, at a country estate, with old friends and cold drinks.

By the 1980s, Polo Ralph Lauren was a billion-dollar brand. The polo shirt was its foundation. Lauren had taken a sport shirt and turned it into something that worked in a boardroom, at a barbecue, on a date, and on a golf course. No other garment in history has been so versatile.

The Democratisation of the Polo Shirt

What makes the polo shirt extraordinary is how far it has travelled from its origins. It began in the exclusive world of horse polo — a sport that, even today, is played mostly by the very wealthy. But the shirt itself became the opposite of exclusive. It became the most democratic garment in the world.

Fred Perry — the British tennis champion — launched his polo shirt brand in 1952. It was adopted by the Mod subculture in 1960s London, then by skinheads, then by the Northern Soul scene, then by Britpop bands. The same shirt that Ralph Lauren sold as a symbol of old-money elegance, Fred Perry sold as a symbol of working-class cool. The polo shirt held both meanings without contradiction.

"The polo shirt is the only garment that can be worn ironically and sincerely at the same time. It means wealth and it means rebellion. It means conformity and it means independence. It is whatever you need it to be."

In hip-hop culture, the polo shirt took on yet another meaning. In the 1980s and 1990s, crews like the Lo Lifes in Brooklyn collected and wore vintage Ralph Lauren Polo as a form of street luxury — taking a garment designed to signal old-money privilege and recontextualising it as a badge of urban style. The polo shirt absorbed this meaning too, without losing any of its others.

Two Billion Shirts a Year

Today, the polo shirt is manufactured by thousands of brands in virtually every country on earth. It is the uniform of golf clubs, corporate offices, school dress codes, and weekend barbecues. It costs three dollars at a market stall in Mumbai and three hundred dollars at a Ralph Lauren boutique on Madison Avenue. It comes in cotton, polyester, silk, linen, and performance fabrics. It comes in every colour, every size, and every price point.

And all of it — every single polo shirt on every body on every continent — traces back to a group of riders on horseback. Persian warriors training for battle. Indian horsemen hitting a willow ball with a stick. British officers in stiff collars, sweating in the Indian sun, wishing someone would invent a better shirt.

Someone did. And the world has been wearing it ever since.

The sport of kings gave the world its most democratic garment. Two billion polo shirts a year — and every one of them carries the ghost of a horse.
