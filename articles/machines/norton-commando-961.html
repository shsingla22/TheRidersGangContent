Norton Commando 961: The Last of the Hand-Built British Twins

By The Rider's Gang | March 8, 2026 | 8 min read
OriginSolihull, England
Engine961cc air-cooled parallel twin
PowerApprox. 77 hp
Indicative PriceFrom approx. £16,000 / $20,000

Standing in front of a Norton Commando 961, you realise you are not looking at a product. It sits low and long on wire wheels, tank narrow at the knee, silencers running back on either flank like the barrels of a well-kept shotgun. It looks less like something engineered to a brief than something that arrived from another decade and was kept up.

That impression is not an illusion. The 961 is genuinely old-fashioned where it matters — air-cooled, pushrod-operated, built by hand in small numbers — and modern where it counts, with Öhlins suspension and Brembo brakes. It also carries a new weight: Norton confirmed in 2025 that the 961 had reached the end of its run, and unveiled a wholly new range at EICMA that November. For the first time in the marque's modern history, there is no Commando in the catalogue.

Why This Machine

The industry is very good now at building fast, competent motorcycles and increasingly poor at building memorable ones. Much of what is sold as heritage is a styling exercise laid over a liquid-cooled engine and a corporate chassis — a decision taken late, after the important choices were made. The Norton is the other thing.

Air cooling means no radiator, and no radiator means the engine is the front of the motorcycle: fins visible, cases visible, nothing behind plastic. A parallel twin means a narrow machine, and therefore the low, unbroken line from headstock to tail that no bodywork can fake. Twin shocks mean a classic triangle at the back, not the hump of a monoshock.

So the 961 belongs in the same conversation as a bench-made shoe or a waxed cotton jacket: built the difficult way because the difficult way produces what the easy way cannot. It is one of very few motorcycles you could stand beside a 1970 Commando and see the resemblance in the bone structure rather than the paint.

"The retro character is not applied to the machine. It is the consequence of the machine's architecture — every visual quality the 961 has is paid for somewhere in the engineering."

The Beauty

Start with the proportions. The roadster stance depends on relationships most modern motorcycles have abandoned: a level line from tank to tail, wheels of similar visual weight, an engine that fills the frame, a riding position that lets you sit on the machine rather than crouch over it. Norton did not rediscover these proportions. They are what a British roadster is.

Then the finish. The 961 is assembled by hand, and the evidence is in the parts others would have cheapened: polished and brushed alloy where alloy belongs, yokes machined from billet, brackets finished rather than merely made. The tank's pinstripe is hand-applied — somewhere in Solihull, a steady hand and a lining brush account for the most noticeable line on the motorcycle.

The exhausts deserve their own sentence. Twin silencers run low and long down each flank in the peashooter idiom that has belonged to British twins since before the war: slim, tapering, unapologetically symmetrical. They are among the last arguments that an exhaust is part of the shape of a motorcycle rather than a component to hide beneath it.

Finally, and least fashionably, rarity. Norton never built the 961 in volume — low thousands across its lifetime, not the tens of thousands that define a mainstream model. With production ended, that number is fixed. Every 961 that will ever exist already does.

"A motorcycle built in small numbers by hand is not a limited edition. It is the honest output of a factory that refused to build any other way."

The Power

The numbers plainly, so nobody is misled. The 961cc air-cooled twin makes somewhere near 77 horsepower, with roughly 80 newton-metres of torque available well before it. Bore and stroke are 88mm by 79mm, the crankshaft runs at 270 degrees, and the valves are worked by pushrods with hydraulic lifters. Against a modern middleweight these figures are unremarkable. Against the purpose of the machine, they are exactly right.

Tuning a big twin for peak output is a destructive exercise: you raise the revs at which the engine does its best work, narrow the band in which it feels alive, and trade away the tractability that makes a road bike pleasant on a wet lane in February. Norton went the other way. The 961 pulls from low down in tall gears, unhurried enough that you rarely change down for an overtake.

The 270-degree crank is central to the character. It gives the engine an uneven firing interval — the loping beat between the flat drone of a 360-degree twin and the bark of a V-twin — and lets the rider feel what the rear tyre is doing. At small openings it burbles; at a third throttle in third it hardens into something deep and slightly indignant. It is not loud so much as present, the way a horse is present underneath you.

The History Behind It

Norton begins in 1898, when James Lansdowne Norton set up in Birmingham making fittings for the young two-wheel trade. Within a decade the name was attached to something more glamorous: at the first Isle of Man TT in 1907, a Norton ridden by Rem Fowler won the twin-cylinder class. It set the pattern the marque has followed ever since.

The most important thing Norton ever made was not an engine. At the turn of the 1950s the McCandless brothers produced a duplex loop frame of such poise that Geoff Duke is said to have compared riding it to riding a featherbed — and the Featherbed frame took its name from the remark. When people say the British were beaten on power but never on handling, this is what they mean.

The Commando arrived at the Earls Court Show in 1967, production machines following in 1968. Its 745cc overhead-valve twin developed an already elderly design, and it vibrated. Norton's answer was the Isolastic system, from a team including Dr Stefan Bauer, Bernard Hooper and Bob Trigg: engine, gearbox and swinging arm were mounted together in rubber isolators, so the vibrating assembly moved as one piece and the frame — and therefore the rider — was insulated from it. Readers of Motor Cycle News voted the Commando Machine of the Year five years running, 1968 to 1972.

"The Isolastic system was a slightly heretical answer to a problem the rest of the industry solved by starting again. It worked — for five straight years, it was the best motorcycle in Britain."

It did not save the company, because nothing could have. The engine grew to 828cc in 1973, the year a government-backed merger created Norton Villiers Triumph from the wreckage of the British industry, and by October 1977 Commando production had ended — some sixty thousand machines in all. The name then passed between hands. In Oregon, Kenny Dreer developed an all-new 961 before funding ran out in 2006. In 2008 Stuart Garner acquired the rights and established Norton at Donington Park; that business entered administration in January 2020.

What followed is the most consequential thing to happen to Norton in fifty years. In April 2020 TVS Motor Company of India acquired the Norton assets in an all-cash deal reported at around £16 million, then did the work the brand had needed for decades: it built a factory. A new facility at Solar Park in Solihull opened in November 2021, and the reworked Commando 961 followed in 2023 in Sport and Café Racer forms — Öhlins, Brembo, an engine revised for consistency rather than headline output.

What It Costs

All figures here are indicative and approximate as of early 2026. Pricing varies enormously by market, specification, model year, on-road charges, taxes and import duties. Treat what follows as a way of understanding the 961's position, not as a quotation.

In the United Kingdom, its home market and by far its most straightforward, the Commando 961 was listed at its 2023 launch at roughly £16,500 for the SP and about £17,000 for the CR, before on-the-road costs. That places it well above the mainstream modern classics and into territory held by low-volume specialists.

Elsewhere the arithmetic is less reliable. A rough sterling conversion puts the machine near the twenty-thousand-dollar mark before duty, shipping or homologation, and where it arrives as a fully imported unit those additions are substantial. It costs what a hand-built British twin has always cost: more than the rational alternative.

Where to Find One

Norton's distribution has never been broad, and with the 961 out of production it is narrower still. In most markets the realistic route to one is the used market, remaining dealer stock, or a specialist importer — not a showroom order. Two practical notes. Because the 961 spans more than one ownership era, know which machine you are looking at: a Donington-built example from the 2010s and a Solihull-built 2023-onward bike differ in specification, finish and parts support. And buy on condition and history — on a low-volume motorcycle, service records are worth more than a few hundred pounds of negotiation.

What Makes It Unlike Anything Else

Every manufacturer now sells a heritage motorcycle. Almost all are excellent, and almost none are actually old — they are modern machines in period clothing. The Commando 961 is a stranger thing: a motorcycle whose retro character is structural rather than cosmetic, built by a company declared finished more than once and not finished yet.

What that produces has no true equivalent at any price. There are faster twins, smoother twins, cheaper twins and more sensible twins. There is nothing else combining air-cooled architecture, hand finishing, a lined tank, peashooter pipes, a name with a hundred and twenty-eight years behind it, and a production run that has now closed.

And there is the quieter thing. The 961 assumes its owner will keep it — not use it up, not trade it at three years, but keep it, learn it, hand it on. That is an assumption almost nothing in the modern market will make, and precisely the one a good saddle and a good pair of boots have always made.

The Commando has died before and come back. Whether it returns under Norton's new range is a question for another decade. For now the 961 sits where the best British machines have always sat: slightly behind the times, well ahead of the argument, and looking, in the right light, like something you inherited rather than bought.
