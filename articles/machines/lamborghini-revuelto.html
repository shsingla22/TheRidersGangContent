Raging Bull: The Lamborghini Revuelto and the Case Against Restraint

By The Rider's Gang | March 8, 2026 | 9 min read
OriginSant'Agata Bolognese, Italy
Engine6.5-litre V12 with three electric motors
PowerApprox. 1,001 hp combined
Indicative PriceFrom approx. €500,000 / £450,000 / $610,000

Almost everything else in this collection whispers. The wax jacket that improves as it fades. The riding boot that takes a decade to look right. Restraint is this publication's through-line, less an aesthetic preference than a moral one: the belief that the best things are made well, used hard, and never announced.

And then there is the Lamborghini Revuelto, which announces itself from four streets away.

It has doors that open toward the sky, an engine displayed under glass, a little over a thousand horsepower, and a noise that arrives before the car does. By every rule this site holds dear it should be inadmissible. We are admitting it anyway.

Why This Machine

Old money's quarrel is not with power. It never was: the country house is not a modest building, the thoroughbred is not a modest animal. What old money objects to is borrowed power — the badge worn to imply capability nobody earned. The Revuelto is not borrowed. Every decibel is backed by something real happening behind the driver's head.

There is a version of the modern supercar that is essentially costume: heavily turbocharged, digitally smoothed, engineered to feel dramatic while asking little of the person steering. The Revuelto is the opposite argument. Lamborghini had every commercial reason to abandon the naturally aspirated V12 — expensive, heavy, hard to certify, no longer needed for big numbers. They kept it, then built a hybrid architecture around preserving it.

"Restraint is a virtue only when there is something to restrain. The Revuelto is not vulgar because it is loud. It is loud because it is genuinely, structurally, mechanically enormous."

There is also the matter of nerve. This publication has always had a weakness for things made by people who refused to be sensible — the jacket-maker who would not modernise, the bootmaker who still cuts by hand. Sant'Agata belongs to that lineage. So we do not defend the Revuelto by pretending it is subtle. We admit it because raw engineering ambition, honestly pursued and fully paid for, is its own kind of integrity — and because a collection that only admires quiet things stops being a collection and becomes a mood board.

The Beauty

Begin with the doors, because everyone else does. Lamborghini has hung its twelve-cylinder flagships on upward-swinging hinges since the Countach entered production in the mid-1970s — a signature roughly half a century old, unbroken through the Diablo, Murciélago, Aventador and now the Revuelto. It is one of the few pieces of automotive theatre to become genuinely traditional. A Lamborghini V12 without scissor doors would be a hunting jacket without a game pocket.

The exterior language rests on one repeated shape — the Y. It appears in the daytime running lights, again in the tail lamps, again in the hexagonal intake and vent geometry Lamborghini has used as a house motif for decades. The car reads as a single idea rather than a collection of surfaces, and in low light the lighting signature alone identifies it — as close as a car gets to a monogram.

Behind the cabin the engine sits under glass. Most manufacturers now bury their powertrains beneath acoustic covers, on the reasonable grounds that a modern engine bay is not beautiful. Lamborghini made the V12 the centrepiece of the rear three-quarter instead, framed like an exhibit: we are not embarrassed by what is under here.

Inside, the reference is aeronautical. The cabin wraps the driver in a narrow, high-sided cockpit, the start control under a hinged cover, three digital displays across the fascia. Beneath it all sits a carbon fibre monocoque, front structure in composite too: the part nobody sees, the most expensive thing in the room, and the reason everything above it can be so extreme.

The Power

The Revuelto is the first hybrid V12 in Lamborghini's history, and the important word is hybrid, not V12. The twelve-cylinder is not the compromise here. It is the thing being protected.

At the centre is a new 6.5-litre naturally aspirated V12 — not a carried-over Aventador unit but a fresh design, rotated relative to its predecessor so the dual-clutch gearbox could sit behind it rather than ahead. It breathes without assistance, so power arrives the old way: progressively, relentlessly, at crankshaft speeds most modern engines never approach. Peak power lands well beyond 9,000 rpm, a figure only a handful of road cars on sale can match.

Around it sit three electric motors: two on the front axle, one per wheel, and a third integrated with the transmission at the rear. A compact lithium-ion battery occupies the central tunnel where a propshaft would once have run. Combined output is quoted a little over a thousand horsepower — Lamborghini's 1,015 CV converts to roughly 1,001 hp — with a claimed 0–100 km/h of about 2.5 seconds and a top speed given as over 350 km/h.

"The electric motors in a Revuelto are not there to make the V12 less necessary. They are there to make it possible to keep."

That is the honest reading of this powertrain. The front motors deliver instant torque at precisely the moment a naturally aspirated engine has least to give, and because they drive each front wheel independently, the car turns with a precision no 1,000 hp mid-engined machine has any business having. None of it replaces the V12's job; all of it covers the V12's weaknesses, so the engine never has to be turbocharged, downsized or apologised for. Electrification here is not a concession to the future. It is a defence of the past, paid for with the future's technology.

The History Behind It

Ferruccio Lamborghini, born in 1916 to farming people in Emilia-Romagna, spent the war as a mechanic and afterwards made his money in tractors. By the late 1950s he was a wealthy industrialist buying the cars such men bought — including a Ferrari.

Here the legend begins. The story, retold for sixty years, is that Ferruccio found the clutch in his Ferrari unsatisfactory, carried the complaint to Enzo Ferrari himself, and was told that a man who builds tractors should not presume to advise a man who builds racing cars. It is a wonderful story, and should be treated as exactly that: a widely repeated account whose details have never been independently established. Confrontation or not, Automobili Lamborghini was founded in 1963 at Sant'Agata Bolognese, its first engine a V12 designed by Giotto Bizzarrini.

The defining moment came three years later. The Miura placed a V12 transversely behind the driver in a road car — a layout borrowed from racing and, until then, thought unsuitable for the street. It was fast, beautiful, imperfectly engineered and without precedent, and it set the template every mid-engined supercar since has followed. Lamborghini did not invent the fast car; it invented its shape.

"The Miura wrote the grammar of the supercar. The Countach then spent twenty years shouting it. Every V12 Lamborghini since has had to answer to both."

Then came the Countach — a concept in 1971, in production from 1974, wearing Marcello Gandini's wedge and the upward-opening doors that became the marque's permanent signature. The line ran on through the Diablo from 1990, the Murciélago from 2001 and the Aventador from 2011: four decades of naturally aspirated twelve-cylinder flagships built by a company that changed hands repeatedly. Chrysler owned it around the turn of the 1990s, Indonesian investors after that, and in 1998 it passed to Audi within the Volkswagen Group. German capital did not sand the character off Sant'Agata; it funded the V12 long after a smaller independent would have stopped.

The name follows the house rule: Lamborghini names its cars after fighting bulls. In Spanish the word also carries a sense of the scrambled and turbulent — which, for a car mixing twelve cylinders with three electric motors, is either coincidence or a very good joke.

What It Costs

A note on every figure that follows: it is indicative only, approximate as of early 2026, and it moves constantly. Supercar pricing varies enormously by market, specification, currency, taxes and import duty. Treat it as an order of magnitude, not a quotation.

Broadly, the Revuelto starts somewhere around €500,000 in Europe, roughly £450,000 in the United Kingdom, and in the region of $610,000 in the United States — all before local taxes, delivery charges and, crucially, options.

The options are not a footnote. Lamborghini's Ad Personam programme allows near-limitless personalisation of paint, trim and carbon detailing, and a specified car routinely lands materially above its headline price.

Then there is time, the other currency. Flagship V12s are built in small numbers, and demand has outstripped supply since the Revuelto was revealed in 2023. Waiting lists have run to years in most markets, with allocations offered first to existing customers. Buying one is less a transaction than a queue you must be admitted to — which, for a certain buyer, is the point.

Where to Find One

The Indian figure deserves an honest explanation rather than a shrug. India levies steep customs duty on fully-built imported vehicles above a certain value threshold, with further levies and state registration charges stacked on top. The effect is that a Revuelto in Mumbai or Delhi can approach double its European equivalent by the time it is on the road. That is not dealer margin; it is tax policy — and the fact that buyers there still pay it says something about the pull of the badge.

Used examples surface occasionally through specialist brokers in London, Dubai and Southern California, though early cars have generally traded at or above list. Provenance, specification and service history determine value far more than mileage does.

What Makes It Unlike Anything Else

The naturally aspirated twelve-cylinder engine is, in the mid-2020s, close to extinct: emissions regulation, efficiency targets and the cost of certification have removed it from almost every catalogue. Lamborghini not only kept its V12 but spent enormously to design a new one and build a hybrid system whose purpose is to let that engine survive another decade. There is no commercial logic to this, only conviction, which is rarer.

Then there is the completeness of the thing. The Revuelto is not an engine wrapped in bodywork: it is a carbon monocoque, a bespoke twelve-cylinder, a rotated driveline, a torque-vectoring electric front axle and a matrix of drive modes running from silent electric crawl to full-noise track configuration — all conceived together. Very few cars at any price are designed this comprehensively from a blank sheet.

And there is the honesty of its intent. The Revuelto does not pretend to be practical, discreet or reasonable. In a market thick with cars performing seriousness, there is something restful about one that simply is what it appears to be.

The riding tradition this publication comes from understands a version of this. A hunter is not a sensible animal; a racehorse is not a sensible animal. They are temperamental, single-purpose creatures kept by people who love them for precisely the qualities that make them impractical, and nobody has ever apologised for a thoroughbred. The Revuelto is Sant'Agata's: bred for one job, named after a fighting bull, wholly indifferent to whether you approve. It is the loudest object we will ever put in this paddock, and it has earned the stall.
