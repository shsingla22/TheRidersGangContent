The Wax Jacket: How Barbour Dressed a Nation of Riders

By The Rider's Gang | March 7, 2026 | 6 min read

There is a particular shade of green that belongs to the English countryside. Not the bright, shouting green of new grass, but a darker, quieter tone — the colour of hedgerows after rain, of moss on dry stone walls, of a land that has been walked and ridden and worked for centuries. It is also, not coincidentally, the colour of a Barbour jacket. The two have become so intertwined that to see one is to think of the other, as though the jacket were not manufactured at all but simply grew out of the landscape itself.

For more than a hundred and thirty years, Barbour has been clothing the people who move through that landscape — on horseback, on motorcycles, and on foot. It has dressed monarchs and farmers, aristocrats and labourers, point-to-point spectators and dispatch riders in wartime. It has earned Royal Warrants and Hollywood fame, and through it all it has remained, stubbornly and beautifully, the same thing: a waxed cotton jacket built to keep the weather out and let the rider carry on.

"A Barbour jacket is not fashion. It is infrastructure. It belongs to the land the way a gate belongs to a field — functional, weathered, and entirely indispensable."

1894: South Shields and the Sea

John Barbour was a Scotsman who settled in South Shields, a port town at the mouth of the River Tyne in northeast England. In 1894, he opened a small business supplying oilskin and waterproof clothing to the sailors, fishermen, and dockworkers who made their living on the North Sea. These were men who needed protection from weather that could kill — horizontal rain, freezing winds, salt spray that rotted ordinary cloth in weeks. Barbour's oilskins kept them dry. They were heavy, they smelled of linseed and paraffin, and they worked.

The business grew slowly, as serious businesses do. John Barbour's sons joined, then their sons after them. The company stayed in South Shields. It stayed family-owned. And in the early twentieth century, it made the material innovation that would define it forever: waxed cotton.

Waxed cotton was lighter than oilskin, more breathable, and far more versatile. A jacket made from it could be worn all day in driving rain and still keep you dry. It was tough enough for brambles and barbed wire, soft enough to move with you in the saddle. It developed a patina over time — scratches, creases, areas where the wax wore thin and needed reproofing — that made each jacket unique to its owner. A Barbour jacket didn't just age. It matured.

The Rider's Jacket

Barbour's connection to riding — both horse and motorcycle — runs through the company's history like a bridle path through a wood. In the equestrian world, the Barbour jacket became standard issue for anyone who spent time around horses. It was the jacket you wore to muck out stables at dawn, to hack through muddy lanes, to stand at the rail of a point-to-point in February when the wind came straight off the hills and the ground was ankle-deep in mud. It was the jacket that could take a soaking, dry overnight on the back of a kitchen chair, and be ready again the next morning.

The equestrian connection gave Barbour something invaluable: credibility. This was not a jacket designed in a studio and marketed with lifestyle photography. It was a jacket that smelled of horse and wet dog and woodsmoke, that had hay in its pockets and mud on its hem. It was tested not by focus groups but by weather, and it passed every time.

But it was motorcycling that gave Barbour its most dramatic chapter. In 1936, the company created the Barbour International — a one-piece waxed cotton suit designed specifically for motorcycle trials riders. The International was built for the kind of riding where falling off was not a possibility but an inevitability: scrambles, enduros, the International Six Days Trial. It had to protect the rider from rain, mud, cold, and abrasion, while allowing the freedom of movement needed to wrestle a heavy motorcycle through terrain that would defeat a horse.

"Every great riding jacket begins the same way — not with a sketch, but with a problem. Barbour's problem was the North Sea. Its solution clothed a nation."

The International suit was a masterpiece of functional design. It became the standard garment for British motorcycle trials throughout the mid-twentieth century. Almost every major trials rider of the era wore one. And it established Barbour as a brand that understood motorcycles not as fashion accessories but as machines ridden by serious people in serious conditions.

The Royal Warrant and the Old Money Uniform

Barbour holds three Royal Warrants — from Her late Majesty Queen Elizabeth II, from the Duke of Edinburgh, and from the Prince of Wales. The Queen was famously photographed wearing her Barbour jacket while walking her corgis at Balmoral, while watching horse trials, while doing the things she did when she was not being the Queen but simply a woman who loved horses and dogs and the Scottish countryside. The jacket she wore was not new. It was visibly worn, reproofed many times, the olive green faded in places to a soft, pale khaki. It was, by any measure, the most prestigious wax jacket in the world, and it looked like it had been through a hedge backwards.

This is the paradox at the heart of Barbour's appeal, and the reason it became synonymous with a particular strain of British wealth. In a culture where old money distinguishes itself from new money precisely by not displaying it, the Barbour jacket is the perfect garment. It is expensive enough to signal belonging — a new Beaufort or Bedale is not cheap — but its genius is that it looks better the more battered it becomes. A pristine Barbour, still stiff with fresh wax, marks you as a newcomer. A Barbour that has been worn for fifteen years, the corduroy collar fraying, the game pocket stained, the wax worn smooth at the elbows — that marks you as someone who belongs.

"New money buys a Barbour. Old money inherits one. The jacket knows the difference, and so does everyone at the point-to-point."

The jacket became the unofficial uniform of the British upper-middle class — the county set, the Pony Club mothers, the land agents and estate managers and weekend shooters. It appeared at Badminton and Burghley, at Cheltenham and the Highland Games. It was draped over the back seats of Land Rover Defenders and hung on hooks in boot rooms from Cornwall to Caithness. It was, and remains, a garment that communicates a very specific message: I am comfortable here. I have always been comfortable here.

Steve McQueen and the American Discovery

If the Royal Warrant gave Barbour its establishment credentials, Steve McQueen gave it cool. McQueen, the self-styled "King of Cool," was an obsessive motorcycle racer who competed in the International Six Days Trial in 1964. He wore the Barbour International suit. The photographs — McQueen mud-splattered and grinning, goggles pushed up onto his forehead, the waxed cotton dark with rain — did for Barbour in America what decades of quiet British countryside wearing had done at home. They made the jacket look like the most desirable garment on earth.

Barbour was shrewd enough to recognise the gift McQueen had given them. Decades later, they launched the Steve McQueen collection, a line of jackets and clothing inspired by the actor's racing years. It brought a new generation to the brand — people who had never mucked out a stable or ridden a motorcycle in the rain, but who understood that the Barbour jacket carried a weight of authenticity that no designer label could manufacture.

The McQueen connection also highlighted something important about Barbour's dual identity. Here was a jacket equally at home on a horse rider at a country show and on a motorcycle racer at a trials event. The waxed cotton did not discriminate between saddles. It simply kept the rider dry and let them get on with it.

The Modern Barbour: Still Between Two Saddles

Today, Barbour remains family-owned, still based in South Shields, still manufacturing waxed cotton jackets that are built to be reproofed and worn for decades. The company has expanded into fashion collaborations and lifestyle collections, but the core product — the Beaufort, the Bedale, the International — endures largely unchanged.

What makes Barbour remarkable in the modern age is the way it continues to occupy the space between horse riding and motorcycle culture, between country tradition and urban style, between function and fashion. A Barbour jacket is worn by riders who still need it to do its job — keeping them dry on a hack through January mud or on a motorcycle ride through autumn rain — and by city dwellers who have never sat on anything with four legs or two wheels but who recognize the jacket as something real in a world of synthetic gestures.

The wax cotton still needs reproofing. The jackets still smell faintly of that distinctive Barbour smell — lanolin and cotton and something indefinably outdoors. They still crease and wear and soften with use. They still look better at twenty years old than they do at twenty days old. And they still carry, in every stitch and seam, the memory of John Barbour's original purpose: to make a jacket that could stand between a person and the weather, and win.

In the end, that is why Barbour endures. It is not a brand that followed fashion. It is a brand that solved a problem — for sailors, then for riders, then for a nation — and kept solving it, quietly, beautifully, and without ever feeling the need to raise its voice. Like the best of British things, it simply got on with it. And the riders, on horseback and on motorcycles alike, got on with it too.
