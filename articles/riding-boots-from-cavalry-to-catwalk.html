Riding Boots: From Cavalry to Catwalk

By The Rider's Gang | March 4, 2026 | 5 min read

Every part of a riding boot exists because of the horse. The tall shaft protects your calf from the stirrup leather rubbing against it. The smooth sole slides easily into the stirrup. The low, angled heel stops your foot from slipping through. The thick panel on the inside of the knee grips the saddle.

It was built purely for function. But over five hundred years, this practical piece of equipment became one of the most lasting fashion statements in history. From Roman soldiers to the 2026 runways — the riding boot has walked a longer road than any other shoe.

Ancient Origins

Soldiers on horseback have worn tall boots for thousands of years. Roman cavalry riders used leather boots to protect their legs in battle. Medieval knights wore armoured boots for the same reason. The boot and the horse have been partners since people first rode into war.

1817: The Duke of Wellington Changes Everything

The real turning point came in 1817. The Duke of Wellington — the British general who defeated Napoleon at Waterloo — went to his shoemaker with a problem. Fashion was changing. Men were starting to wear trousers instead of knee-breeches, and the fancy boots of the time didn't fit under them.

The boot his shoemaker created was a game-changer: calfskin leather, no decorative trim, close-fitting around the leg, mid-calf height, and a simple one-inch heel. It was clean, practical, and good-looking. Within a year, every fashionable man in England wanted a pair.

"Wellington understood that a boot must work before it can look good. The boot his shoemaker made for him did both perfectly."

The Wellington boot set the template for the modern riding boot: clean lines, a heel for the stirrup, and tall enough to protect the leg. It even got a second life in 1852 when rubber versions were made — over 1.1 million pairs went to the trenches in World War One.

India's Gift: The Jodhpur Boot

While Europe was perfecting the tall boot, India created something different — the jodhpur boot. It was ankle-height with a strap and buckle, named after the city of Jodhpur in Rajasthan.

What made it special was its versatility. Unlike tall riding boots that only worked with riding breeches, the jodhpur boot looked good with everyday clothes. It brought riding style to the street — an idea that would prove hugely important a century later when equestrian fashion hit the mainstream.

The Modern Masters

Ariat — Named after Secretariat, the famous Triple Crown winner. Founded in 1993 by two Stanford classmates who applied Nike-style sports technology to riding boots. Today it's the world's biggest equestrian sports brand.

De Niro Boot Co. — Italian craftsmen who put over 200 hand operations into every single pair. There's a great story behind their tradition: after a struggling rider found a one-cent coin hidden in his boot lining and then won his class, De Niro started hiding a lucky penny inside every pair they make.

Parlanti Roma — The gold standard of custom riding boots since 1987. Made from naturally tanned Italian leather by artisans who've been doing this for three decades. Each pair is as much a work of art as it is riding equipment.

2025–2026: The Runway Moment

Right now, the riding boot is having its biggest mainstream moment since Wellington made it fashionable in 1817. Ralph Lauren's Pre-Fall 2025 collection featured tall boots with jodhpurs. Chanel's Fall 2025 show had thigh-high boots in forest colours. Hermès' Spring 2026 collection included riding-style knee-high boots in 55 out of 57 looks. Online searches for riding boots are up 260% year-on-year.

But here's what sets the riding boot apart from other fashion trends: it's not really a trend at all. It's functional equipment that people have worn continuously for over five hundred years. The riding boot is older than the fashion industry itself. The horse hasn't changed. The boot, in its basic form, hasn't changed either. What changes is the world noticing it again.

Five centuries. From battlefield to barn to runway. The riding boot was never designed to be fashionable — which is exactly why it always is.
