Riding Boots: From Cavalry to Catwalk

By The Riders Gang | March 4, 2026 | 8 min read
[ Editorial Image: Classic Tall Riding Boots ]

Every boot tells a story about the horse it was made for. The high shaft that protects the calf from the stirrup leather. The smooth sole that slides easily into the iron. The low, angled heel that prevents the foot from slipping through. The reinforced inner knee panel that grips the saddle. Every detail of the riding boot exists because of the horse. And yet, somehow, this most functional of objects has become one of the most enduring fashion statements in history.

From the Roman cavalry to the Duke of Wellington's personal cobbler, from the battlefields of Waterloo to the runways of Chanel and Ralph Lauren — the riding boot has walked a longer road than any other piece of footwear. This is that journey.

The Cavalry Boot: Ancient Origins

The relationship between boots and horses is as old as mounted warfare. The Roman caligae — hobnailed military boots — were worn by cavalrymen and foot soldiers alike. By 301 AD, they were being sold to civilians across the Roman Empire, marking one of the earliest examples of military footwear crossing into everyday use.

But the true ancestor of the modern riding boot emerged in the Tudor period (1500s-1600s), when thigh-high leather boots were first worn with buff coats by gentlemen and cavalry soldiers. By the reign of Elizabeth I, these boots featured low heels specifically designed to keep the rider's foot secure in the stirrup. The Cavalier boot — soft, knee-high, made of brown calfskin — became the standard for mounted soldiers and remained in military use until the late eighteenth century.

The Hessian Boot: A Prussian Import

In the eighteenth century, a new boot arrived from the German states. The Hessian boot — first worn by soldiers from the Landgraviate of Hesse — was popularised by Prussian King Frederick the Great and became the boot of choice for light cavalry regiments, particularly hussars. It featured polished leather, a distinctive V-shaped notch at the front of the top line, ornamental tassels, a low heel, and a semi-pointed toe designed for easy stirrup use.

The Hessian crossed from military to civilian life during the Regency period (1811-1820), becoming the fashionable boot of the English gentleman. It was elegant, structured, and unmistakably equestrian in origin — the first riding boot to become a genuine fashion item.

The Duke of Wellington's Boot

In 1817, Arthur Wellesley, the first Duke of Wellington — the man who had defeated Napoleon at Waterloo two years earlier — visited his personal shoemaker, Hoby of St James's Street, London, with a specific request. Male fashion was shifting from knee-breeches to trousers, and the ornate Hessian boot could not be worn beneath them. Wellington also had a practical complaint: boots worn outside legwear on military campaign became damp and were difficult to remove.

The boot Hoby created was a masterwork of functional design. Made from calfskin leather, it eliminated the Hessian's decorative trim. It fitted more closely around the leg. It stopped at mid-calf rather than the knee. And it had a lower heel — approximately one inch — that was practical for both riding and walking.

"Wellington was a soldier who understood that a boot must work before it can look good. The boot Hoby made for him did both — and young men across England were wearing it within a year."

The Wellington boot — adopted immediately by young men eager to emulate their war hero — became the most influential boot design in history. Its silhouette established the template for the modern riding boot: close-fitting, clean-lined, heeled for the stirrup, and tall enough to protect the leg.

The Wellington's second life came in 1852, when Hiram Hutchinson — using Charles Goodyear's 1844 rubber vulcanisation patent — began manufacturing rubber versions of the Wellington design. These became indispensable for farmers across Europe. During World War I, the North British Rubber Company (later renamed Hunter Boot Ltd) produced over 1.1 million pairs of rubber Wellingtons for the British Army's trenches.

The Jodhpur Boot: India's Gift to Riding Fashion

While Europe was refining the tall boot, India was creating something different. The jodhpur boot — named after the city in Rajasthan — was an ankle-height boot designed to be worn with jodhpur-style riding breeches. Its low profile and strap-and-buckle closure made it practical for daily wear as well as riding, and it became one of the first equestrian styles to move naturally and effortlessly into mainstream fashion.

The jodhpur boot's significance lies in its democratisation of the riding aesthetic. Where tall boots required breeches and a certain formality, jodhpur boots could be worn with ordinary clothing. They brought equestrian style to the street — a crossover that would prove to be immensely influential a century later.

The Modern Masters

Today, three brands define the art of the riding boot at its highest level — each with a story as distinctive as the boots they create.

Ariat: Named After a Champion

In 1993, two Stanford Business School classmates — Beth Cross and Pam Parker — founded Ariat International. The name was borrowed from Secretariat, the legendary Triple Crown-winning racehorse, and the logo features three horseshoes representing each leg of the Triple Crown.

Cross and Parker had an insight that seems obvious in retrospect: athletic shoes had been revolutionised by companies like Nike and Reebok, with cushioning, stability systems, and ergonomic design. But riding boots had barely changed in a century. They applied athletic-shoe technology to equestrian footwear — incorporating midsoles, cushioning footbeds, and stability shanks (their proprietary ATS Technology) into traditional riding boot designs.

The result was a boot that felt as comfortable as a running shoe but performed like a traditional riding boot. By 2015, Ariat became an official sponsor of the FEI (International Federation for Equestrian Sports), and the company is now the largest equestrian sports brand in the world.

De Niro Boot Co.: The One-Cent Lucky Charm

In 1981, Filippo Donadeo and his wife Irene founded De Niro Boot Co. in Casarano, in the Salento region of Italy — the "heel" of the Italian geographical boot. For the first fourteen years, they produced boots for luxury fashion houses including Prada and Gucci. When those brands moved production east in the mid-1990s, the Donadeos pivoted to equestrian boots, driven by Filippo's personal passion as a reiner.

Each pair of De Niro boots involves over 200 precise hand operations. But the detail that has made the brand beloved is a charming tradition: after a struggling rider discovered a one-cent coin lodged inside his boot's lining and then won his class, De Niro began adding a one-cent coin inside every pair as a good-luck token. It is a small detail — but it captures something essential about the intimacy between a rider and their boots.

Parlanti Roma: The Bespoke Standard

Founded by Renato Parlanti in Rome in 1987, Parlanti immediately established itself as the gold standard for custom-made riding boots. Using naturally tanned Italian leather and blending over three decades of artisanal craftsmanship with modern technology, Parlanti creates boots that are as much personal sculpture as functional equipment. The brand has expanded from bespoke-only to include ready-to-wear collections (Parlanti Passion) while maintaining the handcrafted quality that made its reputation.

The Catwalk: 2025-2026

The riding boot is having its most prominent moment in mainstream fashion since — well, since the Duke of Wellington made it fashionable in 1817. And the reasons are strikingly similar: a cultural desire for heritage, functionality, and understated quality.

Ralph Lauren's Pre-Fall 2025 collection sent models down the runway in tall boots paired with hand-painted jodhpurs and leather hunting jackets, tapping directly into the "horse girl" aesthetic that has overtaken social media. Chanel's Fall 2025 haute couture featured thigh-high boots in forest hues, inspired by founder Coco Chanel's love of the Scottish countryside. Hermès' Spring 2026 ready-to-wear — 55 of 57 looks — featured the same riding-style knee-high boot with an asymmetrical "Spanish topline" collar.

Beyond the luxury houses, the riding boot trend has reached the high street. ASOS reported a 260% year-on-year rise in riding boot searches. TikTok's #horsegirlaesthetic has surpassed 200 million views. The "Year of the Horse" in the Chinese zodiac (2026) has given the trend additional cultural resonance.

But here is what separates the riding boot from other fashion trends: it is not a trend. It is a piece of functional equipment that has been continuously worn for over five hundred years. It predates the fashion industry. It predates the concept of fashion as we understand it. The riding boot was designed for a horse, and the horse has not changed. The boot, in its essential form, has not changed either.

What changes is the world's willingness to see it. And right now, in this moment of quiet luxury and heritage dressing, the world is seeing the riding boot with fresh eyes — and recognising what riders have always known: there is nothing more beautiful than a boot that was built to do a job.

Five centuries. From battlefield to barn to runway. The riding boot was never designed to be fashionable — which is exactly why it always is.
