Belstaff: The Jacket That Built a Riding Legend

By The Riders Gang | March 4, 2026 | 9 min read
[ Editorial Image: Belstaff Trialmaster Jacket ]

There is a photograph of Ernesto "Che" Guevara, taken in 1952, sitting on a Norton 500cc motorcycle somewhere on the roads of South America. He is wearing a Belstaff jacket. There is another photograph — this one more famous — of Steve McQueen on the set of a 1964 film, astride a Triumph TR6 Trophy, wearing a Belstaff. And there is a less known image, from 1948, of a woman named Mary Kenyon-Slaney riding her motorcycle through the Scottish Highlands, wearing the same brand of waxed cotton jacket that her fellow riders had sworn by for two decades.

Belstaff did not invent the motorcycle jacket. But it perfected the idea that a jacket could protect you from everything the road throws at you — rain, wind, gravel, cold — while looking like something you'd actually want to wear when you got off the bike. This is the story of how a textile company in the English Midlands became the most iconic name in motorcycle outerwear.

"Belstaff jackets have been worn on every great motorcycle adventure of the twentieth century. They have become part of the machine."

Stoke-on-Trent, 1924

The story begins in the Potteries — the industrial heartland of Staffordshire, England, where the air smelled of kiln smoke and the economy ran on ceramics and textiles. In 1924, a textile manufacturer named Harry Grosberg founded Belstaff in the town of Stoke-on-Trent. The name combined "Bel" (a phonetic shortening of a partner's surname) with "staff" (for Staffordshire).

Grosberg's innovation was not a design revolution. It was a material one. He developed a method of treating Egyptian cotton with wax to create a fabric that was waterproof, breathable, and remarkably tough. The technique — waxed cotton — had existed in rudimentary forms for centuries, used by sailors and fishermen. But Grosberg refined it for a new purpose: protecting motorcyclists.

In 1920s Britain, motorcycling was booming. The motorcycle was not yet a recreational vehicle — it was the working person's transport, the affordable alternative to a car. But British weather was merciless, and existing riding gear was either too heavy (oil skins), too fragile (standard cotton), or too expensive (leather). Grosberg's waxed cotton hit the sweet spot: it was light, it was waterproof, and it was affordable.

The Trialmaster: A Jacket for the Worst Roads on Earth

By the 1940s and 1950s, Belstaff had become the go-to brand for competitive motorcyclists. The International Six Days Trial — known as the "Olympics of motorcycling" — was the ultimate endurance test: six days of gruelling off-road riding across mountains, rivers, and mud. Riders needed gear that could survive conditions that would destroy ordinary clothing.

In 1948, Belstaff introduced the Trialmaster jacket, designed specifically for these endurance events. The Trialmaster was built around the demands of competitive riders: a four-pocket design for carrying maps and tools, a belted waist that could be tightened to prevent flapping at speed, a high collar against wind and rain, and the signature waxed cotton shell that could take a beating and come back for more.

The Trialmaster became the jacket of choice for riders competing in the Scottish Six Days Trial, the International Six Days Enduro, and the legendary International Trophy Team events. It was not designed to look good. It was designed to survive. The fact that it looked extraordinary — oil-dark, road-scarred, and utterly purposeful — was a bonus that would prove to be Belstaff's greatest commercial asset.

Che Guevara's Motorcycle Diaries

In January 1952, a 23-year-old Argentine medical student named Ernesto Guevara set out on a journey that would change his life — and, eventually, the history of Latin America. Together with his friend Alberto Granado, Guevara embarked on an 8,000-kilometre motorcycle trip across South America on a 1939 Norton 500cc nicknamed "La Poderosa" (The Mighty One).

Guevara wore a Belstaff jacket for the journey. The trip — later immortalised in his memoir The Motorcycle Diaries — took the two young men through Argentina, Chile, Peru, Colombia, and Venezuela. The motorcycle broke down in Chile, and they continued much of the journey by other means. But the image of young Guevara on the Norton, in his Belstaff, became one of the defining photographs of twentieth-century adventure.

"The motorcycle was not just transport. It was freedom. And the jacket — the jacket was the armour that made that freedom possible."

What makes the Belstaff connection significant is that it was not a product placement or a sponsorship deal. Guevara chose the jacket because it was, quite simply, the best motorcycle jacket available. It could handle the rain of Patagonia, the dust of the Atacama Desert, and the humidity of the Amazon basin. The fact that it later became an icon of revolutionary romanticism was an accident of history.

Steve McQueen and the Cool Factor

If Guevara gave Belstaff its revolutionary credentials, Steve McQueen gave it something equally powerful: cool. McQueen — actor, racing driver, and motorcycle obsessive — was photographed repeatedly in Belstaff jackets throughout the 1960s and 1970s. His most famous association with the brand came through The Great Escape (1963), in which McQueen performed many of his own motorcycle stunts wearing a jacket that, while not an official Belstaff in the film, cemented the visual connection between the actor and the brand.

Off-screen, McQueen was a genuine motorcycle racer who competed in events including the International Six Days Trial in 1964 — wearing, naturally, a Belstaff. His team finished in the top third of competitors, and McQueen described it as one of the proudest accomplishments of his life. The photographs from that event — McQueen covered in mud, grinning through exhaustion, his Belstaff jacket bearing the scars of six days of brutal riding — became some of the most reproduced images in motorcycle culture.

McQueen understood something about Belstaff that many fashion brands would later try to replicate: the jacket looked best when it was beaten up. A new Belstaff was handsome. A worn Belstaff — oiled, scratched, softened by years of riding — was magnificent. It told a story. Every scratch was a road. Every stain was a journey.

The Café Racer Connection

While Belstaff was outfitting endurance riders and Hollywood adventurers, another motorcycle subculture was brewing in the streets of London. In the late 1950s and early 1960s, the café racer movement emerged around establishments like the Ace Café on the North Circular Road.

The Ton-Up Boys — so named because they aimed to hit 100 miles per hour — would race between cafés on stripped-down motorcycles, their machines modified for speed rather than comfort. The uniform was distinctive: leather jacket, jeans, engineer boots, and a white silk scarf. But many café racers also wore Belstaff waxed cotton, particularly for longer rides and in the rain. The brand occupied a unique space: too functional to be purely fashionable, too stylish to be purely utilitarian.

The café racer aesthetic — lean, fast, purposeful — influenced generations of motorcycle fashion. When designers like Tom Ford and Hedi Slimane began incorporating motorcycle elements into luxury fashion in the 2000s and 2010s, they were drawing on a visual language that Belstaff had helped create half a century earlier.

A Century of Road Stories

Belstaff celebrated its centenary in 2024. Over those hundred years, the brand has experienced the full arc of British manufacturing — boom, decline, near-death, and revival. Production moved overseas. Ownership changed hands multiple times. There were years when it seemed like Belstaff might become just another heritage brand, preserved in museums rather than worn on roads.

But the jacket endured. The Trialmaster is still in production. The waxed cotton — now supplemented by modern technical fabrics — is still the signature material. And the connection to motorcycle culture remains genuine, not performative. Belstaff still sponsors riding events. It still consults with active riders on design. The jacket is still built to be ridden in.

What Belstaff understood from the beginning — what Harry Grosberg intuited in his Stoke-on-Trent workshop in 1924 — is that the best riding gear is the gear that becomes part of you. A Belstaff jacket does not hang in a closet. It lives on the back of a chair, draped over a motorcycle seat, thrown over the shoulder as you walk into a café. It accumulates the patina of your riding life. And in that accumulation, it becomes something more than clothing. It becomes a record of every road you have ever taken.

A hundred years of mud, wind, rain, and open road — stitched into waxed cotton and still riding.
