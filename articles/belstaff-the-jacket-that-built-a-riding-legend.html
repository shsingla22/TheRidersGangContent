Belstaff: The Jacket That Built a Riding Legend

By The Riders Gang | March 4, 2026 | 5 min read
[ Belstaff Trialmaster ]

Che Guevara wore one across South America in 1952. Steve McQueen wore one racing motorcycles in the Scottish mud. A woman named Mary Kenyon-Slaney wore one through the Scottish Highlands in 1948. The jacket was always a Belstaff.

Belstaff didn't invent the motorcycle jacket. But it perfected the idea that a jacket could protect you from everything the road throws at you — rain, wind, gravel, cold — while looking like something you'd actually want to wear when you got off the bike.

"Belstaff jackets have been worn on every great motorcycle adventure of the twentieth century. They have become part of the machine."

Stoke-on-Trent, 1924

Harry Grosberg founded Belstaff in Staffordshire, England — the name blending a partner's surname with "staff" for Staffordshire. His innovation was material, not design: he treated Egyptian cotton with wax to create fabric that was waterproof, breathable, and tough.

The timing was perfect. 1920s Britain was a motorcycle nation. The bike was the working person's transport, but British weather was brutal and existing gear was either too heavy, too fragile, or too expensive. Grosberg's waxed cotton hit the sweet spot.

The Trialmaster: Built to Survive

In 1948, Belstaff introduced the Trialmaster — designed for the International Six Days Trial, the "Olympics of motorcycling." Four pockets for maps and tools. Belted waist to prevent flapping at speed. High collar against the elements. Waxed cotton that could take a beating and come back for more.

It wasn't designed to look good. It was designed to survive. That it looked extraordinary — oil-dark, road-scarred, and utterly purposeful — was the bonus that became Belstaff's greatest commercial asset.

McQueen Made It Cool

If Guevara gave Belstaff revolutionary credentials, Steve McQueen gave it cool. He competed in the International Six Days Trial in 1964 — wearing a Belstaff, naturally. The photos of McQueen covered in mud, grinning through exhaustion, his jacket bearing the scars of six days of brutal riding, became some of the most reproduced images in motorcycle culture.

McQueen understood something important: a Belstaff looked best beaten up. A new one was handsome. A worn one — oiled, scratched, softened by years of riding — was magnificent. Every scratch was a road. Every stain was a journey.

"The motorcycle was not just transport. It was freedom. And the jacket was the armour that made that freedom possible."

From Café Racers to Couture

In 1960s London, the Ton-Up Boys raced between cafés at 100 mph on stripped-down motorcycles. Many wore Belstaff waxed cotton. The café racer aesthetic — lean, fast, purposeful — influenced generations of motorcycle fashion. When Tom Ford and Hedi Slimane later incorporated motorcycle elements into luxury fashion, they were drawing on a visual language Belstaff helped create.

Belstaff celebrated its centenary in 2024. The Trialmaster is still in production. The waxed cotton is still the signature material. And what Harry Grosberg intuited in 1924 remains true: the best riding gear is the gear that becomes part of you. A Belstaff doesn't hang in a closet. It accumulates the patina of your riding life. And in that accumulation, it becomes something more than clothing — a record of every road you've ever taken.

A hundred years of mud, wind, rain, and open road — stitched into waxed cotton and still riding.
