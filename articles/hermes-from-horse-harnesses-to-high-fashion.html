Hermès: From Horse Harnesses to High Fashion

By The Riders Gang | March 4, 2026 | 5 min read

In 1837, a German-born craftsman named Thierry Hermès opened a small workshop on the Grands Boulevards of Paris. He didn't sell handbags or scarves. He made harnesses and bridles for horses — and he made them better than anyone else in Europe.

Nearly two centuries later, his house is valued at over $200 billion. Its waiting lists stretch for years. Its most famous creation — a leather handbag — sells for more than a new car. But step inside any Hermès boutique today and you'll find something remarkable: the horse is still there. In the logo, in the leatherwork, in the DNA of the brand.

"The horse is the founding myth of Hermès. It is in every stitch, every buckle, every piece of leather we touch."

From Saddles to Handbags

Thierry's son Charles-Émile moved the workshop to 24 Rue du Faubourg Saint-Honoré — still Hermès' spiritual home — and expanded into saddles. The Hermès saddle became the gold standard for equestrians across Europe.

Then came the automobile. For a company built on horse equipment, this could have been fatal. But the third generation made a brilliant pivot: take the leather skills honed over decades of saddlery and apply them to a new world of travel and personal luxury.

In 1922, Émile-Maurice Hermès introduced the first Hermès handbag — adapted directly from a saddlebag design. The fastening mechanisms, the leather quality, the hand-stitching — all of it came from the equestrian workshop.

The Kelly: An Accidental Icon

In 1935, Hermès created a trapezoidal leather bag with a turn-lock clasp. It was designed for carrying saddle equipment. For two decades, it was well-regarded but unremarkable.

Then in 1956, Princess Grace of Monaco used it to shield her pregnant belly from paparazzi. The image circled the world. The bag became the Kelly.

"The Kelly was never designed to be a fashion statement. It was designed to carry riding equipment. That Grace Kelly chose it — that is serendipity."

Here's what makes it extraordinary: each Kelly requires 18 to 24 hours of hand-stitching by a single artisan. The saddle stitch — two needles passing through the same hole from opposite directions — is the exact technique developed for horse harnesses in the 1800s. If one thread breaks, the other holds. It was engineered for the violence of horse riding. It's why a Kelly lasts a lifetime.

The Birkin: Sketched on a Sick Bag

In 1984, Hermès chairman Jean-Louis Dumas sat next to actress Jane Birkin on a flight. Her straw bag spilled everywhere. He asked what she wanted in a bag. He sketched the design on an airplane sick bag.

The result: 48 hours of single-craftsman work. Hand-selected leather. Individually plated hardware. And always — the saddle stitch connecting it back to that original workshop. Today, rare Birkins sell at auction for over $400,000, outperforming the S&P 500 over 35 years.

The Horse Never Left

While other luxury houses chase logos and hype, Hermès keeps the horse at its centre. The Duc carriage logo. The annual Saut Hermès show jumping competition at the Grand Palais. Active saddle and bridle production. The equestrian fashion trend sweeping 2025-2026 runways — Ralph Lauren's jodhpurs, Chanel's riding-inspired couture, TikTok's 200-million-view #horsegirlaesthetic — traces a direct line back to Hermès.

But here's what the trend-followers miss: for Hermès, the horse was never an aesthetic. It was never a mood board. It was the entire reason the company exists. Every bag, every scarf, every belt buckle carries within it the ghost of a horse harness, stitched by a craftsman on the Grands Boulevards, nearly two centuries ago.

From a harness-maker's bench to the most desired luxury house on earth — and the horse was there for every step.
