Hermès: From Horse Harnesses to High Fashion

By The Riders Gang | March 4, 2026 | 8 min read
[ Editorial Image: Hermès Atelier ]

In 1837, a German-born craftsman named Thierry Hermès opened a small workshop on the Grands Boulevards of Paris. He did not sell handbags. He did not sell scarves. He made harnesses and bridles for horses — and he made them better than anyone else in Europe.

Nearly two centuries later, the house that bears his name is valued at over $200 billion, its waiting lists stretch for years, and its most famous creation — a leather handbag — regularly sells for more than a new car. But step inside any Hermès boutique today and you will find something remarkable: the horse is still there. In the logo, in the leatherwork, in the very DNA of the brand. This is the story of how the world's most exclusive luxury house was built on the back of a horse.

"The horse is the founding myth of Hermès. It is in every stitch, every buckle, every piece of leather we touch."

The Workshop on the Grands Boulevards

Thierry Hermès was born in Krefeld, Germany, in 1801. He arrived in Paris as a young man and apprenticed with harness makers, learning the art of working with leather at the highest level. By 1837, he had established his own atelier, specialising in hand-crafted harnesses and bridles for the carriage trade. His clients were the aristocrats and nobility of Paris — people who demanded nothing less than perfection for their horses.

The timing was exquisite. Mid-nineteenth-century Paris was a city that moved on horseback. The carriage was the ultimate status symbol, and the quality of one's harness spoke volumes about one's standing in society. Hermès harnesses became renowned for their exceptional craftsmanship, winning prizes at the Expositions Universelles in Paris. In 1855, Thierry's work was awarded first prize at the Paris Exposition — a distinction that would set the standard for everything the house would create in the decades to come.

Charles-Émile and the Saddle Revolution

When Thierry's son, Charles-Émile Hermès, took over in 1880, he moved the workshop to 24 Rue du Faubourg Saint-Honoré — the address that remains Hermès' spiritual home to this day. Charles-Émile made a pivotal decision: he expanded beyond harnesses into saddles. The Hermès saddle became the gold standard for equestrians across Europe and beyond, prized for its ergonomic design and extraordinary durability.

But Charles-Émile saw something else on the horizon. He began selling finished leather goods to the riders who came for saddles — small bags to strap to their saddles, leather cases for carrying personal items on horseback. It was the first, quiet step away from purely equestrian equipment and toward the luxury goods empire that would follow.

The Automobile Arrives — and Hermès Adapts

By the early twentieth century, the writing was on the wall. The automobile was replacing the horse-drawn carriage. For a company built entirely on equestrian craftsmanship, this could have been an extinction event. But the third generation — Émile-Maurice Hermès, Charles-Émile's son-in-law — made the decision that would define the house's future: he would take the leather skills honed over decades of saddlery and apply them to a new world of travel and personal luxury.

In 1922, Émile-Maurice introduced the first Hermès handbag. The design was not arbitrary — it was born directly from a request by his wife, who complained that she could not find a bag she liked. He adapted the design of a saddlebag used by riders, creating what would become the template for some of the most desired objects in fashion history. The fastening mechanisms, the quality of the leather, the devotion to hand-stitching — all of it came directly from the equestrian workshop.

The Kelly Bag: Grace and the Horse

In 1935, Hermès created a leather bag with a trapezoidal shape, a flap top, and a distinctive turn-lock clasp. It was called the Sac à dépêches and was designed for carrying saddle equipment. For two decades, it remained a well-regarded but not particularly famous accessory.

Then, in 1956, Princess Grace of Monaco — the former Hollywood actress Grace Kelly — was photographed by paparazzi using the bag to shield her pregnant belly from camera flashes. The image circled the world. Overnight, the bag became the most talked-about accessory in fashion. By 1977, Hermès officially renamed it the Kelly bag in her honour.

"The Kelly was never designed to be a fashion statement. It was designed to carry riding equipment. That Grace Kelly chose it — that is serendipity."

What makes the Kelly extraordinary from a craftsmanship perspective is that it requires a single artisan approximately 18 to 24 hours to create, entirely by hand. The saddle stitch used — where two needles pass through the same hole from opposite directions — is the very same technique Hermès artisans developed for stitching horse harnesses in the 1800s. A machine stitch uses a single thread that, if broken, unravels. The Hermès saddle stitch uses two threads. If one breaks, the other holds. It was engineered for the violence of horse riding. It is why a Kelly bag can last a lifetime.

The Birkin: Born on an Airplane, Raised in the Saddle

The Birkin bag — arguably the most famous handbag in history — was born in 1984, and its origin story is as improbable as the waiting lists it would inspire. Jean-Louis Dumas, then chairman of Hermès, found himself seated next to the actress and singer Jane Birkin on a flight from Paris to London. Her straw bag spilled its contents across the floor. Dumas asked her what she wished a bag could do. Birkin described something practical, spacious, and elegant — something she could use every day.

Dumas sketched the design on an airplane sick bag. The result was the Birkin — a structured, rectangular bag with a flap top, a lock, and the unmistakable Hermès saddle-stitched leather. It takes a single craftsman approximately 48 hours to create one Birkin bag. The leather is selected and cut by hand. The hardware is plated individually. And the stitch — always the saddle stitch — connects it back to that original harness workshop on the Grands Boulevards.

Today, a standard leather Birkin retails for approximately $10,000 to $15,000. Rare versions — in crocodile, ostrich, or Himalaya leather — have sold at auction for over $400,000. The Birkin has become not just a fashion item but an investment asset, outperforming the S&P 500 over the past 35 years.

The Horse Never Left

What sets Hermès apart from every other luxury house is not just the craftsmanship — it is the refusal to abandon its origins. While other brands have pursued aggressive expansion and logo-driven marketing, Hermès has kept the horse at its centre.

The Hermès logo — the Duc carriage with horse and driver — has been used since the 1950s. The house still manufactures saddles, bridles, and equestrian equipment at its workshops. The Hermès Saut Hermès, an annual show jumping competition held at the Grand Palais in Paris, is one of the most prestigious equestrian events in the world. In 2025, Hermès expanded its equestrian collections, introducing new saddle lines and riding apparel that bridge the gap between functional riding gear and the quiet luxury the brand is known for.

The Quiet Luxury of the Equestrian World

In 2025 and 2026, the fashion world has embraced what critics call "quiet luxury" — a rejection of loud logos in favour of understated quality, heritage materials, and timeless design. No brand embodies this philosophy more naturally than Hermès, because it was never performing quiet luxury. It was simply making horse equipment, very well, and letting the quality speak for itself.

The equestrian fashion trend that has swept runways — from Ralph Lauren's Pre-Fall 2025 jodhpurs and tall boots to Chanel's riding-inspired Fall 2025 haute couture — traces a direct line back to Hermès. The horse-girl aesthetic trending on TikTok (over 200 million views on the #horsegirlaesthetic hashtag) is, in many ways, the mainstreaming of a sensibility that Hermès has embodied for 189 years.

But here is what the trend-followers miss: for Hermès, the horse was never an aesthetic. It was never a mood board or a hashtag. It was the entire reason the company exists. Every bag, every scarf, every belt buckle carries within it the ghost of a horse harness, stitched by a craftsman in a workshop on the Grands Boulevards of Paris, nearly two centuries ago.

From a harness-maker's bench to the most desired luxury house on earth — and the horse was there for every step of the journey.
